# Lark & Nib’s Treasure Quest

## Season 6: The Workshop of Almost

Writing plan · October 30, 2026 · 12 chapters · Meaningful prefixes/suffixes, spelling changes, and composing a sentence

Planned complete-episode target: **13–15 minutes**, including normal child responses. This is a conditional writing allowance, not measured runtime or a reason to extend a tired child’s session. Use the shorter earlier-season target whenever engagement calls for it.

This is a mini-novel plan and backward-design reference. The [episode briefs](season-6-episode-briefs.md) provide the individual scene and interaction plans. The [series roadmap](series-roadmap.md) holds cross-season rules and the cumulative teaching ledger. These replace older prototype story placeholders for writing; they do not change the deployed games.

## The starting state

The garden works and its visitor note is readable. Neighbors ask the crew for a portable story-making station that can move between camp, garden, and Storyboat landings. An unused makers’ workshop contains unfinished prototypes, a real hand press, and a door smaller than Nib’s first design. The crew accepts four visible requirements: portable, dry, readable, and usable together.

## Plan the ending first

A folding story press rolls through the workshop door, reaches the garden reading place, and produces a short chosen page with a clear sentence. Its source sheets, ink, and stamp plates are physically accounted for; it does not duplicate complete books by magic. All four original requirements are demonstrated. Nib keeps a small workshop stamp, the shared plans remain available, and The Workshop of Almost becomes book six.

## The mini novel

Nib is thrilled by a workshop full of almost-finished inventions. Lark wants to finish one useful thing rather than rescue every prototype at once. Their first design is too large, its handles compete with its paper feed, and a continuous paper roll makes no page anyone can hold. Through successive revisions, they distinguish actions undone and redone, useful and unhelpful parts, finished and ongoing work, and labels whose endings change their meaning. The middle problem is not a missing tool but a poor fit between the invention and its users. A working model becomes a portable folding station only after they test it against the original door and ask whose hands need which job. The child then creates a short real message. The finale tests the complete station in the place it is meant to serve, not just on the workshop floor.

## Work backward from that ending

1. The finale needs a portable, dry, readable, cooperative station; keep those four requirements visible from the opening.
2. Portability needs the original door measurement and folding joints, not a conveniently enlarged door at the end.
3. The printed sentence needs an actual author, source sheet, ink, and readable chosen words; supply them before the final demonstration.
4. Spelling-change applications must follow their explicit lessons; running/tapping and hoping are not retroactively treated as old simple ing words.
5. Repeated rework should fix a known observed problem, not create unrelated repairs to pad the season.

## Character movement

Nib learns that finishing a smaller useful invention can be more satisfying than naming a magnificent impossible one. Lark learns to share design decisions rather than silently perfect them alone. The child becomes a maker of messages, using word parts for meaning as well as pronunciation and choosing a sentence that serves an actual reader.

## Object, information, and destination ledger

- Four requirements are established in 1 and checked in 12; none is replaced with a new final test.
- The door opening is measured at the start and keeps the same dimensions.
- The old press, folding frame, handles, paper roll, cut sheets, dry cover, ink tray, and source plates remain distinct parts with one coherent arrangement.
- A completed printed page consumes or uses visible supplies. Existing library books remain available and are not overwritten by the child’s practice sentence.

## Earlier setups and later payoffs

- The too-large opening plan becomes the folding frame that fits the same door.
- The endless paper roll explains why an invention must produce a page a person can actually use.
- The helpful/unhelpful handle test becomes a cooperative demonstration at the finale.
- The child’s own sentence, rather than a prewritten congratulation, is the last page printed.

## Teaching and review handoff

Every chapter carries the entire earlier taught set forward. Its listed review is a selection, not a reset. New sound/spelling models precede child use. Words can be read as soon as their mappings are introduced; no later “blending” chapter postpones that work. The cumulative ledger in the series roadmap records the exact introduction points for patterns and explicit function words.

The example lists are deliberately limited child-reading targets. A character name, chapter title, spoken sound-bank word, or narrated instruction is not automatically decodable. Narrator-supplied names and untaught text stay supplied. Capital forms are paired explicitly with familiar lowercase forms before they are required. Actual responses guide support; completion is not mastery.

For each new-code chapter, model the focus and use its sound bank disproportionately in natural prose. For comprehension chapters, state the text strategy and continue selective decoding review. Use expressive speech attributions frequently; audition the intended delivery in the recording. Check the entire drafted child-readable passage against the ledger, not only these examples.

## Physical staging and production

Use an accessible workshop floor, fixed door frame, low bench, paper-storage shelves, and a broad garden landing. Every press lever and roller attaches to its frame. Folding parts have pivots and clear travel; nobody stands on an unfolding unsupported panel.

Before generating a plate, specify foot/prop contact planes, attachment points, walking corridors, and moving-platform parents. Composite the real layers at tablet crops. Mouth-only speech and body-only walking remain the motion style. Plan scenes around actual supporting geometry; do not reuse an attractive river plate by placing Nib on its water.

Choose a story-audio budget from the total target after reserving ordinary response and transition time. Measure an expressive read-through, then the recording and played chapter. Never insert extra practice or hurry speech just to meet a duration. New audio requires its own alignment and cuts. Scripts, art, recordings, and builds remain separate production work.

## Resolution and next-season invitation

The completed story press is tested and available for use; its plans and bound book join the library. A quiet change in the garden brook becomes a new question for the field-note season, after the making story has closed.

## Writing acceptance

Before a script is recorded, verify that each opening follows the previous end state, each chapter resolves its own question, and each destination follows from something actually observed or read. Check the ledger, review opportunities, independent/support distinctions, and setup/payoff chain. No unprepared word, new magical power, or hidden assessment threshold may appear only at the finale.
