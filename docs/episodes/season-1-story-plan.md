# Lark & Nib’s Treasure Quest
## Season 1: The Map Pieces

Story plan and writing reference · October 30, 2026

Ten chapters: the existing **The Moon Path**, followed by nine planned stories. Each remaining episode is designed for **8–10 minutes including normal child turns**. This document establishes the season arc and learning sequence; the companion [Season 1 Episode Briefs](season-1-episode-briefs.md) supplies the scene-level plans for writing the individual scripts.

The narrated Moon Path manuscript is the continuity source. Its current 21:27 recording remains unchanged. Earlier Reading Trail screenplay material and unbuilt episode titles are provisional references, not additional events that happened to these characters. The plot below adds new story decisions without changing the opening's established events.

## The season in one sentence

Following the moonstone's light, Lark, Nib, and the child recover the missing sections of an explorer map, learn to read its small clues, and discover that the long-sought Treasure Tree is the great oak at their own camp—whose hidden room has a place for the story they have just lived.

## The ending we are working toward

The completed map leads back to Explorer Camp. At first Lark thinks they have gone in a circle. Nib notices that the tree on the map is drawn from behind: its forked branch and crooked root belong to their own oak. They have been searching for a distant tree while living above its secret room.

On the far side of the oak, the map identifies a narrow gap beneath the crooked root. Its overhang and trailing vines have hidden the seam from ordinary walking and flying views. Behind them, an old brass flap carries the word **tap**. The child reads it and taps the flap. A shallow drawer opens, marked **map**. The child distinguishes **map** from **mat** on the crew's two closed, similarly shaped folios and places the completed map in the drawer. The moonstone, resting in a fitted lamp above it, lights the map's joined paths. The concealed door releases.

Inside are real treasures: a small golden acorn for the child's collection, beautifully illustrated books left by earlier explorers, and a large folding woodland atlas. Beside them is an empty shelf with a plain book cover. Lark and Nib's journal pages belong there. They bind their ten chapters into **The Map Pieces**, read back a little of their adventure with the child, and place the book beside the earlier explorers' stories. The secret room becomes camp's new library room; their book can be borrowed and read again whenever they wish.

The Treasure Tree mystery is resolved. The golden acorn is kept. The book is finished. A woodland path in the atlas offers somewhere to go another day; enjoying the ending does not depend on starting another season.

## The whole mini novel

The opening has already given the crew a mystery and a way to pursue it. Lark has long wanted to find the secret room in the Treasure Tree. Nib hopes treasure will be large, shiny, and possibly edible. The child becomes the third explorer. After the ferry adventure, the moonstone reveals an orchard on the surviving map, and an apple rolls into camp.

The apple leads into the first movement: **following a clue before the whole route is known**. In the orchard, a broken apple-catching awning threatens an old route box. Repairing the awning protects the box and earns the first missing map section. It points to a ridge waystation. To reach it, the crew must make Nib's improvised sail safe in a windy hollow, then steady a tipping table before opening its jammed map drawer. They recover the second section. The map is becoming readable, but the adventure is also teaching them how to travel together.

The middle movement changes the problem: **protecting and using what they have found**. Rain threatens the fragile paper. Nib builds a simple protective map folio instead of his enormous proposed contraption. At the river, they use the properly moored ferry again. On its island, an old ink-box mechanism can only be opened by following its short written instructions in order. The third map section is inside. The familiar river now measures how much more capable the crew has become; there is no second drifting-boat rescue.

The last outward movement is about **paying attention when the goal seems close**. A nesting bird is asleep beside the narrow route to the meadow. The crew quiets the wagon instead of barging through. At the meadow, a red ribbon marks two old animal waymarkers. Nib almost follows the ram, but the tiny written clue says **rat**. Reading the whole word leads to the correct cache and the last missing map section.

In the penultimate chapter, all the pieces are available, but a folded edge makes the route seem to stop in the wrong place. Rather than pressing on or inventing a missing piece, the crew settles under its makeshift shelter and repairs the map properly. Its completed route leads home. Nib, who once ran ahead, is now the one who pauses to inspect the drawing. Lark, who wanted a perfect plan before leaving, can now recognize that the seemingly wrong destination may be the right discovery.

The final chapter takes them around the familiar oak to its unfamiliar side. Their smallest learned words help open its largest secret. The story ends with treasure in the case and their own mini novel on a shelf.

## Character movement

**Lark** remains capable, curious, warm, and fond of maps. Her difficulty is wanting the full route before acting. The season gives her repeated chances to use a small trustworthy clue, revise an assumption, and accept Nib's practical contribution. Her low point is the completed map pointing back to camp. Her payoff is recognizing that a map can change how one sees a familiar place.

**Nib** remains an earnest, inventive mouse with a red scarf, slipping goggles, and a substantial interest in snacks. His difficulty is acting on the first appealing idea. His inventions are useful as often as they are funny: the apple catcher, safe sail, map folio, quiet wagon, and final shelter all depend on him. By the meadow he waits for the end of a word; by the return to camp he notices what rushing would have hidden.

**The child** supplies information and actions the crew needs: identifying sounds, combining them into words, reading a useful instruction, and helping reconstruct their journey. The child is neither a spectator nor a person being examined by the characters. Support stays available, and the adventure continues after a supported response.

Neither adult character is always correct. Lark misreads the island's ink stain and initially dismisses the route home. Nib contributes sound practical ideas. Their growth appears through choices and callbacks, without a closing moral speech.

## Cumulative reading sequence

The planned new mappings are **m → a → s → t → p → i → n → r**, with short a as in *apple* and short i as in *ink*. Episodes 9–10 consolidate and apply this set. The opening order m, a, s, t is retained; p moves ahead of the old tentative r/i order so **map** becomes readable sooner. The later unbuilt seasons will need to inherit this taught set when their plans are revised.

| Ep. | New material | Selected review | Child-readable words available for this episode | Reading's story purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | m /m/ | None assumed | No independent word reading expected | Match the hum to the letter and the useful clue |
| 2 | a /æ/; model the first blend | m | **am** | Contribute **am** to the crew's shared journal line after catching the apples |
| 3 | s /s/ | m, a; **am** | **am** reviewed; no forced new word | Use the /s/ clue to choose the sand sack and make the sail safe |
| 4 | t /t/; grow two sounds into three | m, a, s | **at, mat, sat** | Build/read **mat** to obtain the table support |
| 5 | p /p/ | m, a, t; **mat** | **map, tap, pat**, plus prior words | Distinguish the folios marked **map** and **mat**; peg the right one around the paper |
| 6 | i /ɪ/ | a versus i; m, s, t, p | **it, sit, sip, tip**, plus prior words | Follow **sit**, then **tap**, to open the ink box |
| 7 | n /n/ | i/a; p/t/m | **in, pin, pan, tin, nap**, plus prior words | Read the tool-pocket labels to retrieve **pin** rather than **tin** |
| 8 | r /r/ | a; final t/m; **map** | **ram, rat, ran**, plus prior words | Read **rat**, including the last sound, to find the correct waymarker |
| 9 | No new sound; transfer and join | All eight, with extra review chosen from actual responses | **map, mat, pin, pan, sit, sat, ran**, as useful | Join and secure the map; co-read the journey without new spelling demands |
| 10 | No new sound; purposeful reading and retelling | **tap/map/mat**; selected **pin/ran/sat** | Previously taught words in a new setting | Open the flap, choose the map folio, and co-read the finished chapter |

There is no suitable new ordinary word to force from m/a/s alone in episode 3; that episode still introduces s and reviews the word **am**. Blending has already begun in episode 2. Meaningful three-sound words arrive in episode 4, rather than waiting for a later chapter named “Blending.”

### What the child reads, and what the storyteller reads

The spoken mini novel remains rich prose. **Lark, Nib, moon, apple, sail, table, pocket, island, ribbon, tree**, and other story words are narrator vocabulary unless their untaught parts are supplied. They are not independently readable merely because the child has heard them.

The child's printed reading targets use lowercase and only the mappings in the table. Adult narration supplies names, capitals, function words, and the connective prose in shared reading. For example, the adult can read *Nib found the…* while the child reads **map**. Do not quietly make **the**, article **a**, or the names into new sight-word requirements. Short phrases and sentences can grow in later seasons as their necessary patterns are taught.

The word lists record planned instruction, not a claim that Connor has learned them. Use actual first responses to select review and support. A supported blend remains supported participation. No speed, accuracy score, or mastery lock is added to the adventure.

## Map, object, and rule continuity

### Exactly five map sections

The map begins as one surviving central section with the Treasure Tree drawing and the known lagoon/woods/river route. It has four missing sections. The exact four gaps are made clear in episode 2, without claiming the opening already counted them.

| Section | Found | Adds | Kept afterward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Original | Existing episode 1 | Tree drawing, lagoon, woods, river, first hidden orchard lead | Explorer case; then protected folio |
| A: orchard edge | 2 | Route through the windy hollow to the ridge waystation | With the original; not left at the orchard |
| B: river edge | 4 | Ridge-to-river route and island cache location | Protected with A and the original in 5 |
| C: meadow edge | 6 | Island-to-meadow route past the quiet nesting bank | Joins the protected stack |
| D: return edge | 8 | Final connection and reverse-view root detail | All five sections assembled in 9 |

The sections were kept in the old explorers' route boxes after the map wore at its folds. This is new information supplied by the orchard box's picture note, read aloud by Lark. The crew is restoring an old route, not chasing a map that inexplicably tears again at the end of every episode. The storm in the opening explains damaged paths and signs; it is not retrospectively made responsible for every object in the season.

### Rules that stay the same

- The moonstone reveals hidden ink on existing paper. It does not invent missing routes, answer spoken questions, or repair damage.
- Lark can fly short scouting trips, as established in the opening. A puzzle must not depend on her forgetting this. She cannot steady a tipping table, hold a large wet cover against the wind, or carry a mouse and a loaded wagon by herself.
- The old explorers used some letter/sound devices. The season also contains ordinary repair, travel, and reading problems. Not every location needs another magic letter lock.
- Nib's wagon, rope, umbrella, picnic basket, journal, explorer case, and moonstone remain with the crew unless a scene deliberately parks or stores them. Transfers are narrated.
- The ferry is correctly moored before boarding and while at the island. Nib is attached visually to its deck when it moves. The opening's drift accident is remembered, not replayed.
- The mat, two labeled folios, clips, sand sack, red ribbon, and journal pages are retained when introduced. Their later reuse is a payoff, not a surprise new power.
- The great oak's branch/root silhouette is consistent from the front and rear views. Its treehouse can be visible in the background of the final approach so the reveal has visual proof.

## Pacing, scenes, and visual planning

Each remaining episode has one main problem, one significant complication, and an earned local resolution. Its ending closes that problem before revealing the next lead. Use approximately four or five short scenes and five meaningful response gates; the precise count serves the story. Optional humming and repeating may share a gate with a touch response rather than adding another delay.

A working nine-minute budget is about **6:45 of recorded story, 1:45 for normal responses, and 0:30 of other transitions**. This is a writing allowance, not measured audio or a response deadline. A timed performance determines the word budget. Shorten prose or redundant turns if the actual complete episode exceeds ten minutes; never rush the clues to fit.

The new sound recurs disproportionately in narration and dialogue as well as prompts. Each episode brief has its own sound bank. Use expressive attributions such as *Lark exclaimed*, *Nib murmured suspiciously*, or *Lark urged gently* while retaining clear, simple prose. The actual recording must be auditioned for the intended performance.

Reuse camp, woods, and river art only where the actual geometry supports the scene. New/adjusted locations are an orchard catching area, ridge waystation, island bank/ink-box shelter, quiet nesting path, meadow waymarkers, rear view of the camp oak, and tree-room interior. Some can be variations of existing plates; reuse is not a reason to place feet over water.

Before background generation, identify each foot/prop support plane, walking corridor, and layer order. Give the orchard catcher a real frame and ground anchors; the ridge table visible legs and a stable floor; the island a broad dry landing and tied ferry; the nest a fixed branch beyond the walkway; the final doorway a continuous ground-level entrance. Review actual composites at the intended tablet crops before accepting them.
