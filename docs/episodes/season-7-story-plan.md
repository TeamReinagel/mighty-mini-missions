# Lark & Nib’s Treasure Quest

## Season 7: The Vanishing Brook

Writing plan · October 30, 2026 · 10 chapters · Reading to learn: observations, diagrams, main idea, evidence, and fresh text

Planned complete-episode target: **14–16 minutes**, including normal child responses. This is a conditional writing allowance, not measured runtime or a reason to extend a tired child’s session. Use the shorter earlier-season target whenever engagement calls for it.

This is a mini-novel plan and backward-design reference. The [episode briefs](season-7-episode-briefs.md) provide the individual scene and interaction plans. The [series roadmap](series-roadmap.md) holds cross-season rules and the cumulative teaching ledger. These replace older prototype story placeholders for writing; they do not change the deployed games.

## The starting state

The story press is working and the garden remains open. One morning the familiar brook beside the garden sounds unusually quiet. The crew brings its field notebook, existing map, small viewing lens, and a borrowed channel diagram. The moonstone remains a fictional ink-revealing object; its behavior is not evidence about real rocks or water.

## Plan the ending first

The crew documents a specific local cause: a branch holds an old gate in the wrong position, diverting the brook into its alternate channel. The keeper clears and resets the supported gate, the usual brook route flows again, and later observations confirm the change. Field Notes records observations, hypotheses, action, and result separately. A small field lens is collected. The novel ends with a solved local mystery and a useful record, not a claim that the child is now an independent scientist.

## The mini novel

The missing sound invites a tempting first story: perhaps the brook has simply vanished. The crew learns to keep the question smaller and observable. A diagram takes them to the inlet and outlet; an older note and a current note seem to disagree; a crowded page hides its useful main point. Lark initially favors the explanation that fits her first impression. Nib notices an overlooked difference, then must learn not to turn every interesting difference into a cause. They read how to inspect without disturbing the evidence, label the actual features, compare sources, and make a prediction. A direct observation finally shows the branch and gate. The repair is followed by another observation rather than an instant celebration standing in for proof. By the last chapter the child can use a fresh note and explain what changed, while admitting what the page does not tell them.

## Work backward from that ending

1. The final explanation requires before/after observations and a tested gate change; establish the record before the repair.
2. The repair requires a directly observed branch/gate relationship; a convincing old note is insufficient.
3. The observation needs the correct inlet/outlet route and clear labels; diagram and label chapters precede the gate visit.
4. A source comparison needs two genuinely different records with stated times, not two pages secretly describing the same moment.
5. The opening records the quiet brook without declaring a magical or seasonal cause.

## Character movement

Lark learns to distinguish a favorite explanation from supported evidence. Nib learns to offer an observation before announcing a conclusion. The child uses reading to answer a real question, asks when text is unclear, and learns that sometimes a page cannot answer without another observation.

## Object, information, and destination ledger

- The original downstream observation is recorded in 1; the diagram is consulted in 2; current inlet/outlet information is checked through 6–7.
- The branch/gate cause is directly confirmed and the keeper’s repair performed in 8. It is not announced as proved in an earlier prediction chapter.
- The ordinary gate changes water routing; neither the moonstone nor the field lens controls water.
- The older dry-weather note and current keeper note retain their stated times; neither is rewritten to make the preferred answer look right.

## Earlier setups and later payoffs

- The familiar brook at the opening is revisited in the same frame at the end.
- The inlet/outlet arrows become the real walking route and then the explanation diagram.
- The wrong early seasonal explanation is revised rather than erased.
- The question the page cannot answer in 9 motivates one final fresh observation instead of an invented fact.

## Teaching and review handoff

Every chapter carries the entire earlier taught set forward. Its listed review is a selection, not a reset. New sound/spelling models precede child use. Words can be read as soon as their mappings are introduced; no later “blending” chapter postpones that work. The cumulative ledger in the series roadmap records the exact introduction points for patterns and explicit function words.

The example lists are deliberately limited child-reading targets. A character name, chapter title, spoken sound-bank word, or narrated instruction is not automatically decodable. Narrator-supplied names and untaught text stay supplied. Capital forms are paired explicitly with familiar lowercase forms before they are required. Actual responses guide support; completion is not mastery.

For each new-code chapter, model the focus and use its sound bank disproportionately in natural prose. For comprehension chapters, state the text strategy and continue selective decoding review. Use expressive speech attributions frequently; audition the intended delivery in the recording. Check the entire drafted child-readable passage against the ledger, not only these examples.

## Physical staging and production

Keep one consistent channel diagram and matching geography: upstream inlet, old gate, alternate channel, usual brook, garden path, and downstream hut. The keeper handles the real gate; the child reads, selects, records, and follows dry paths. Any water change must follow that established geometry. Real-world reading pages need fact checking before recording; the authored scene observations here are fiction.

Before generating a plate, specify foot/prop contact planes, attachment points, walking corridors, and moving-platform parents. Composite the real layers at tablet crops. Mouth-only speech and body-only walking remain the motion style. Plan scenes around actual supporting geometry; do not reuse an attractive river plate by placing Nib on its water.

Choose a story-audio budget from the total target after reserving ordinary response and transition time. Measure an expressive read-through, then the recording and played chapter. Never insert extra practice or hurry speech just to meet a duration. New audio requires its own alignment and cuts. Scripts, art, recordings, and builds remain separate production work.

## Resolution and next-season invitation

The brook mystery is solved and its evidence book is complete. A blank atlas page asks where the next crew-chosen expedition should go. That question opens Season 8 after this investigation has reached its own conclusion.

## Writing acceptance

Before a script is recorded, verify that each opening follows the previous end state, each chapter resolves its own question, and each destination follows from something actually observed or read. Check the ledger, review opportunities, independent/support distinctions, and setup/payoff chain. No unprepared word, new magical power, or hidden assessment threshold may appear only at the finale.
