These nine briefs develop the [season story plan](season-1-story-plan.md) into individual stories ready for prose drafting. They continue the **narrated** Moon Path, whose ending reveals the orchard and sends a rolling apple into camp. They do not continue the older sun-path screenplay prototype.

## Conventions for writing all nine scripts

- Target **8–10 minutes for the whole episode**, including normal responses. A starting allowance is 6:45 of story audio, 1:45 of responses, and 0:30 of other transitions. Time an expressive read-through; no recording has yet verified these budgets.
- Start by showing what the crew brought from the previous chapter. Introduce one main problem. Keep the complication attached to that same problem. Solve it before the next-story invitation.
- Use the sound bank generously in understandable narration and dialogue. Names and rich story vocabulary remain adult-read. Only the listed child-reading words are independent printed targets.
- Model each new mapping before using it in a task. Model blending early. Later independent applications can use a new word with the taught pattern; do not immediately speak the answer and then report the child's echo as independent decoding.
- **R** below means review offered before a hint; it is not proof of mastery. **M** means modeled or guided practice. **A** means a first application without an answer-revealing hint; record only what the response actually demonstrates. **S** means an ungraded story action. Help remains available in every case.
- A tap on a printed word does not prove oral decoding. Label choices can show word recognition; a sound choice can show sound discrimination; oral reading requires an actual spoken response. Optional speech always has a touch-supported continuation. Unsupported microphone behavior must not stop the story.
- At every gate, the recording ends after the prompt. The narrated consequence plays only after the action is settled. A miss brings specific feedback, then another attempt; no timer, failure screen, or extra mandatory drill.
- Preserve narrated context between quotations. Suggested dialogue lines below use the speaker's name immediately after the quotation for compatibility with the current parsing tool. Production instructions in these briefs are not spoken text.
- Add one brief journal page at each ending. This is an illustrated record of the solved adventure, not a second retelling lesson that makes every episode run long.

## Episode 2 · The Apple Catcher

Production update, November 1: [The Apple Catcher](s1e2-apple-catcher-production.md) is built in `apple-catcher/` with original art and the supplied 6:39.670 recording. Its estimated total is about nine minutes including child turns and feedback; actual child-paced timing remains unmeasured. From November 2, 2026 it is Connor’s story-day reading mission in [the two-week plan](two-week-plan-2026-11-02.md). Later chapters below remain authored briefs.

**Story question:** Can the crew stop a tumbling crop of apples from crushing the orchard's old route box?

**Opening state:** The moonstone, surviving map, journal, rope, umbrella, and wagon are at camp. The apple from episode 1 is still beside the wheel. The child has been introduced to m, without an assumption of mastery.

**New teaching:** Lowercase a represents short /æ/ as in apple. Review /m/. Model the first word, **am**, as /æ/ joined to /m/. The narrator supplies *I* and the rest of a shared journal sentence.

**Sound bank:** apple, ant, amber, branches, basket, catch, clap, am, map. Only **am** is a child-reading target; hearing *apple* does not introduce p/l/e.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `problem-solving-1`: follow the simple order of securing the catching cloth before releasing the next apple chute. Keep the same order visible in the picture.

### Scene progression

1. **An invitation with a bite missing.** Nib would like to investigate the camp apple by eating it. Lark notices the moonstone's orchard line and takes the apple as a clue. As the crew leaves, Nib bumps his wheel around the camp oak's distinctive crooked root. He jokes that the tree has its foot out. This is a small observation, not an announcement of the finale.
2. **The broken catcher.** At the orchard, a few apples roll down a wooden gathering chute. Its cloth catcher has come loose at one corner, so apples bounce into a shallow heap around an old route box. A carved apple and lowercase a mark the chute's catch. Lark introduces short /æ/. Nib volunteers as a catching basket and discovers that a mouse has fewer pockets than he needs.
3. **A catcher that stays caught.** Nib stretches the cloth while Lark steadies its frame. The child uses the new sound to find the a catch, then follows an ant-shaped /æ/ clue to the second fastening. The repaired cloth holds the next apples. This is a small supported repair, not a rapid reaction game.
4. **The first missing section.** The crew clears the settled apples and opens the dry route box. Its picture note, read by Lark, explains that earlier explorers kept sections of the worn map in route boxes. The intact map has four empty spaces. Section A fills one and reveals a ridge waystation beyond a windy hollow. In the journal, Lark models **am** in the crew's shared *I am an apple catcher* line; the child contributes the one taught word. Nib's drawing has an apple in every pocket.

**Dialogue anchors:** “I am an excellent apple basket,” Nib declared proudly. “You are a rather small one,” Lark observed gently. After the repair: “I am an apple catcher,” Nib exclaimed, then caught the apple he had been saving for himself.

**Staging:** The chute stands on a real frame with its catcher suspended from visible corners. Both characters stand on the orchard's dry bank; apples settle onto cloth or ground, not in midair. The route box is on a low fixed shelf behind the catcher. No child waits while apples continually fall toward a deadline.

**End state and handoff:** Original map + A; moonstone and journal retained; several apples in the picnic basket. A's hidden ridge line emerges under the moonstone. A gust rattles the wagon's small pennant. Nib looks from the pennant to the cloth and has an idea.

## Episode 3 · The Sail That Wouldn’t Stay

**Story question:** Can Nib's sail help the wagon up the windy hollow without pulling it sideways?

**Opening state:** A shows the route toward the ridge. The crew has the wagon, rope, picnic cloth, apples, and protected explorer case. The map has not gained another section.

**New teaching:** Lowercase s represents /s/. Review m and short a, including **am**. Do not manufacture a new word from the small sound set; this chapter adds s while keeping the first real word in use.

**Sound bank:** sail, sun, sand, sack, soft, safe, settle, small, hiss, moss. Words such as *sail* and *safe* remain narrated; the child's short a does not decode their long vowels.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `planning-3`: work carefully to secure the small sail and place ballast before the wagon is used.

### Scene progression

1. **A splendidly oversized idea.** Nib ties the picnic cloth to the parked wagon. The wind swells it so grandly that he imagines sailing all the way to the Treasure Tree. The wheels are chocked on a broad bank. Lark points out that the sail is pulling the wagon sideways even while it is parked.
2. **The hiss and the sack.** Air slips through the grass with a hiss. Lark introduces s and its sound. A worn supply sack lies beneath a low shelter along the route. Its s patch and the sand bank offer Nib a smaller, more practical idea: a sand sack can steady the wagon.
3. **Less sail, more sense.** The child identifies the sack from the /s/ clue and helps fill it to a visible line. Nib folds the cloth down to a much smaller sail. Lark tests the rope. A gust presses against it; the wagon stays steady. Nib admits that a sail can be splendid without being enormous.
4. **Up to the ridge.** The characters pull and guide the wagon together while the small sail assists. They stop at the ridge waystation, not halfway through an unresolved wild ride. The journal records the safe sail. The child revisits **am** in a short adult-supported line. Inside the shelter, a tapping sound comes from a table that rocks whenever the wind reaches it.

### Child turns

| Gate | Child task | Literacy role | Automatic consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | Read am with optional sound slide | word reading | Resume the sail story |
| 3.2 | Hear s, then choose s among s/m | new sound | Keep the new sound on the page |
| 3.3 | Write s with the guided form | writing | Resume the supply-shelter scene |
| 3.4 | Hear mat/sack, then choose the /s/ object | sound application | Crew brings the sack |
| 3.5 | Fill the sack to its line | one extra story action | Crew ties it low and folds the sail |
| 3.6 | Choose which sail helped the wagon stay steady | comprehension | Resume the safe journey to the ridge |

**Dialogue anchors:** “A sail the size of the sky!” Nib announced grandly. “A wagon the size of a mouse,” Lark replied cautiously. At the ridge: “Small sail. Sensible sack. Splendid sailing,” Nib murmured with satisfaction.

**Staging:** The parked wagon has visible wheel chocks and a dry support plane. Ballast attaches to its frame. The sail's forces are shown by cloth angle and a small safe lean, not by a character or loaded wagon flying through the air. The ridge path is wide enough for the wagon and two helpers.

**End state and handoff:** No new map section. Small sail, rope, and sand sack retained. The crew has reached the waystation indicated by A. Nib puts one paw on the tapping table and watches its far end rise.

## Episode 4 · The Tipping Table

**Story question:** How can the crew open a jammed map drawer without tipping its whole table?

**Opening state:** The crew is at the ridge waystation. A has led them here. A shallow drawer in a four-legged table contains section B, visible through its glassless inspection opening. One short leg makes the frame twist and the drawer bind.

**New teaching:** Lowercase t represents the crisp /t/ sound. Review m/a/s. Join a+t into **at**, then add m for **mat** and contrast **sat**. New sound demonstrations must not add a full “tuh.”

**Sound bank:** table, tap, tiny, timber, twig, tip, tight, at, mat, sat. *Table* is spoken vocabulary, not a newly decodable long-a word.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `problem-solving-1`: support the short leg first, then open the drawer; reverse order fails safely and reveals why order matters.

### Scene progression

1. **The table that answers back.** Each light tap makes the table rock. Nib pulls the drawer, which catches firmly. He proposes an apple under the short leg. It rolls a few inches away, leaving the table as wobbly as before. Lark holds the frame so neither it nor its contents can fall.
2. **A small sound at the end.** Lark models t through the sound at the start of tap and the end of at. She blends **at** on a reusable strip in the crew's journal kit. Add m and the word becomes **mat**—the picnic mat rolled beneath their basket. The child helps make its label and retrieve it.
3. **Fold, test, then pull.** Nib folds one edge of the mat into a firm pad. The child puts it under the short leg, tests the table, then opens the unjammed drawer. Nib does the useful folding; Lark accepts a folded, slightly untidy solution instead of searching for a perfect matching table leg.
4. **The second route.** B is laid beside the original and A. Under the moonstone, its ink draws the way back to the river and a small box on the ferry's island. The table's tiny sketch of the Treasure Tree shows a forked branch; Lark remarks that it looks oddly familiar, without identifying it. She copies **sat** beside a drawing of Nib resting on the steady mat. While Lark holds the table, Nib replaces the temporary folded mat with a flat wooden offcut from the shelter's repair shelf. The table stays steady when they pack their mat and map sections.

### Child turns

| Gate | Prompt and action | Kind | Feedback / narrated consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4.1 | Review s versus m with their sounds before new t teaching | R | Replay the relevant sound model after a miss |
| 4.2 | Model t; tap the t tile among t and s | M | Contrast a short stop with the continuing hiss; no “tuh” model |
| 4.3 | Join **at**, then complete m-a-__ with t to make **mat** | M: cumulative word building | Revisit the final sound and keep the tiles in place; Nib retrieves the rolled picnic mat |
| 4.4 | Choose support-under-leg before pull-drawer, then place the folded mat at the visible short leg | S: supported sequence | Pulling first produces the same small catch; the picture shows the unsupported gap |
| 4.5 | After stability is visible, open the drawer and remove B | S | The drawer slides cleanly; the island route is revealed |

**Dialogue anchors:** “An excellent table, apart from its standing-up,” Nib observed thoughtfully. “Hold this corner,” Lark urged gently. After the repair: “Not even a wobble,” Nib whispered suspiciously, then tapped it once to make sure.

**Staging:** Table legs meet a level shelter floor. Show the actual short-leg gap, the folded mat entering it, and the resulting level tabletop. A few flat offcuts are visible on the repair shelf from the first shot so the permanent support is not conjured at the ending. Nib's test seat or rest is on the floor mat, not on an unsupported floating edge. The map drawer must be reachable after the support is placed.

**End state and handoff:** Original + A + B, picnic mat, sail, sand sack, journal, and moonstone retained. The crew leaves for the river. The first raindrop spots the table's empty surface just as Nib notices that the old paper has a soft, fraying edge.

## Episode 5 · A Pocket for the Map

**Story question:** Can the crew protect its old map from a passing shower without building a contraption too large to carry?

**Opening state:** B supplies the island route. The crew is on a broad dry riverbank beside the moored ferry. It has all three current map sections, the picnic mat, cloth, rope, and a few small packing pegs. The shower is mild and the paper can be sheltered while the child thinks.

**New teaching:** Lowercase p represents /p/. Review t, short a, and m. Blend **map** and compare it with **mat** by attending to the last sound. Use **tap** and **pat** as small supported word-building extensions, not three extra required tests.

**Sound bank:** paper, pocket, peg, pebble, pat, pop, paddle, protect, map, tap. The p model is a light release without an added “puh.”

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `teamwork-1`: the child places a peg while Nib holds the cover and Lark keeps the paper flat.

### Scene progression

1. **A palace for paper.** Rain begins as the crew prepares the ferry. Lark immediately shelters the map beneath the umbrella. Nib sketches a roof, wheels, seven poles, and somewhere for a snack. His design is bigger than the ferry. The problem is real but safe: the map stays covered while they consider a simpler pocket.
2. **The last little pop.** Lark models p with paper, peg, and the end of **map**. The child builds **map** after reviewing **mat**. Changing one final tile changes which thing they mean. This is the story's reason for treating the whole word carefully.
3. **Two sensible folios.** Nib folds spare cloth around a stiff backing to make one map folio; a matching flat cover holds the picnic mat. The crew labels them **map** and **mat**. Their outer shape, color, and fastenings are deliberately similar. The child identifies the map folio by its word and pegs it around the paper. Lark tries one drop on the outside: it rolls off. The protected paper stays dry.
4. **Ready for the island.** The shower passes. They pack both folios, the small clips, and the other supplies. Through the moonstone's light, B's island drawing shows a low shelter and a little ink box. Nib puts one apple into an ordinary pocket and carefully keeps it out of the map pocket. The ferry remains moored for a calm departure next chapter.

### Child turns

| Gate | Prompt and action | Kind | Feedback / narrated consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5.1 | Revisit **mat** before the new letter model | R: practiced word | Help isolates the final /t/; the crew unfolds its picnic mat as a work surface |
| 5.2 | Model p, then choose p among p and t | M | Contrast the two short sounds without adding vowels |
| 5.3 | Change the last tile of m-a-t to p, then join **map** | M: word building | Keep m-a steady while re-modeling the changed final sound |
| 5.4 | “We need the cover for the paper map. Find its word.” Choose the closed **map** folio over **mat** | A: word recognition | “Listen to the last sound in map. This one ends with /t/.” Both labels remain available |
| 5.5 | Place the peg at the visible clasp while Nib holds the fold | S: teamwork | An off-target drop leaves the peg in hand; a valid clasp secures the cover |

**Dialogue anchors:** “A portable palace for precious paper,” Nib proclaimed enthusiastically. “Could our palace fit in a pocket?” Lark asked hopefully. After the drop test: “Perfectly dry,” Nib murmured proudly. “And considerably smaller than a palace.”

**Staging:** Characters and map work are on the bank, not the river surface. The umbrella rests in a visible support or is held. Folios lie on the picnic mat; pegs visibly grip cloth. The ferry's deck and mooring line remain clear even when a cropped tablet view is used.

**End state and handoff:** No new section. Original + A + B are in the map folio; mat in the matching mat folio. Pins/clips and pegs remain in the wagon kit. The island ink box is the next concrete destination.

## Episode 6 · The Little Ink Box

**Story question:** Can the crew work out how to open a heavy little ink box without smearing its clue or tipping its support?

**Opening state:** The paper is dry. B identifies the island shelter. The same ferry from episode 1 is available. The crew checks its rope, travels on the deck, and ties it at the island before anyone steps ashore.

**New teaching:** Lowercase i represents short /ɪ/ as in ink. Review short a by contrasting **sit/sat**, and revisit **tap**. Available new words include **it, sit, sip, tip**. The word *island* is narrated, never offered as a short-i example.

**Sound bank:** ink, insect, inside, little, drip, sip, tip, sit, lid. Untaught letters in words such as *lid* remain adult-read.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `listening-2`: use the simple **sit → tap** order, retained visibly in the scene rather than demanding memory alone.

### Scene progression

1. **A quieter river visit.** Nib remembers his previous drifting trip and checks both moorings with exaggerated care. A brief callback shows growth without replaying the accident. On the island's broad dry bank, the crew finds a low ink-box shelter, a sturdy bench lever, and a brass tap plate.
2. **A clue in a drip.** An ink smear seems to blot out the whole instruction strip. Lark sighs that it may be ruined. Nib notices that the drip has stopped just below two readable words. Lark introduces short i, blends **sit**, and helps compare it with the already-known **sat**. The instruction strip shows **sit → tap**, with the first word above the bench and the second above the plate.
3. **A small instruction with weight behind it.** Standing and pulling does nothing. Nib sits on the bench, which settles the box's counterweight. The child then taps the plate. The little lid lifts, safely braced by its hinge. Nib approves of a mechanism whose first instruction is sitting down. If the plate is tapped first, the mechanism merely remains closed; the visible unweighted lever explains why.
4. **The third section.** Inside lies C, wrapped in dry cloth, and an ink stamp the crew may keep. C shows a narrow path to the meadow beside a nest symbol. The child helps stamp the journal's ink-box page. Lark thanks Nib for looking carefully at what she nearly dismissed. They pack C into the protected folio and untie the ferry only after everyone is aboard for the return.

### Child turns

| Gate | Prompt and action | Kind | Feedback / narrated consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6.1 | Recall **tap** on the crew's practice strip before new i teaching | R | Blend the three known sounds after the first attempt if help is needed |
| 6.2 | Model short i with ink; choose i rather than a | M | Contrast the short vowel sounds using the same printed forms |
| 6.3 | Guide s-i-t into **sit**, then compare it with **sat** | M: word blend and contrast | Keep s/t fixed and re-model the middle sound |
| 6.4 | Use the displayed words in order: select the bench for **sit**, then tap the plate for **tap** | A: applying practiced instructions, with separate first actions | A premature tap leaves the lever raised; feedback points to the first word and empty bench |
| 6.5 | Open the supported lid and place C into the folio | S | The meadow route and journal stamp are revealed |

**Dialogue anchors:** “Rope on. Mouse remaining,” Nib declared firmly. “I thought the ink had hidden everything,” Lark admitted ruefully. “It left the sitting bit,” Nib observed with interest.

**Staging:** Ferry passengers and supplies move with the deck; the hull rail overlaps lower bodies correctly. The shelter, bench, counterweight, and box stand on the island, never over implied open water. Give the bench a fixed pivot and a small bounded travel; the lid cannot overlap Nib's head or float unattached.

**End state and handoff:** Original + A + B + C, ink stamp, folios, pins, rope, and journal retained. The ferry is returned to its normal dock and moored. C points the crew along a quiet nesting bank toward the meadow.

## Episode 7 · The Noisy Wagon

**Story question:** Can the crew get its rattling wagon past a sleeping nest without waking its small resident?

**Opening state:** C supplies the next route. The crew travels on the dry bank after leaving the ferry tied behind. The wagon carries both folios, the journal, and the kit. Its loose metal fitting begins to rattle.

**New teaching:** Lowercase n represents /n/. Review i/a and p/t. Blend **in**, then **pin**; **tin, pan, nap** are available contrasts or supported examples. Nib's name is useful narrated /n/ vocabulary, not independently decodable because b has not been taught.

**Sound bank:** Nib, nest, nose, narrow, nudge, nap, pin, tin, in. Keep the /n/ model distinct from /m/; mouth position can be shown without making pronunciation a lock.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `teamwork-2`: adjust the crew's actions to the quiet nesting place. Do not score tapping pressure or microphone volume as a behavioral skill.

### Scene progression

1. **A rattle with excellent timing.** The route is narrow but safe, with a nest on a fixed branch beyond the path. A small bird is asleep inside. Nib tiptoes with great seriousness; the wagon responds with a clatter. He freezes with his nose in the air. Lark identifies the loose fitting rather than blaming Nib for making noise.
2. **A little n at the end.** Lark introduces n through nest and nap, then joins i-n and p-i-n. Nib remembers the small fastening pins packed with the folios. Their two closed tool pockets have labels **pin** and **tin**. One holds the useful clip; the other holds a small spare metal tin that would not fix the rattle.
3. **Fix the wagon, then travel.** The child recognizes the pin pocket. Nib holds the fitting still while the child clips it to the wagon frame. They roll a short test distance. The fitting stays put. The solution changes the wagon, rather than demanding that a three-year-old perform a perfectly silent gesture.
4. **A quiet arrival.** The crew passes the nest along the visible bank. The bird shifts comfortably and stays asleep; there is no penalty if the earlier attempt rattled. At the meadow, C's final drawing matches a red ribbon on a waymarker. The journal shows Nib tiptoeing and his wagon doing the same, which he considers a very accurate portrait.

### Child turns

| Gate | Prompt and action | Kind | Feedback / narrated consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7.1 | Revisit the middle sound in **sit** before n teaching | R | Compare short i with a, keeping the outer letters visible |
| 7.2 | Model n; select n among n and m | M | Contrast tongue-up /n/ with lips-together /m/; touching still works if the child does not speak |
| 7.3 | Join i-n into **in**, then add p to form **pin** | M | Rejoin the same sounds slowly; do not reset to an unrelated drill |
| 7.4 | “Which pocket has the fastener we need?” Choose **pin** rather than **tin** after the word has been taught | A: practiced word recognition | “This begins with /t/. Our pin begins with /p/.” The labels remain available |
| 7.5 | Attach the pin to the loose fitting and roll the wagon through its short test path | S | The fitting answers with a small settling click and the wagon stops rattling |

**Dialogue anchors:** “I am being exceptionally quiet,” Nib whispered earnestly. His wagon clattered. “Your wagon has another opinion,” Lark murmured gently. After the repair: “Now we are both being quiet,” Nib breathed proudly.

**Staging:** The bird and nest have a fixed branch support outside the walking corridor. No one moves the nest or climbs beneath it. The wagon fitting stays attached to its frame while loose, then is visibly clipped in place. Both labeled pockets have comparable size, color, and placement so the label carries the word choice.

**End state and handoff:** No new map section. The wagon is quiet and the crew has reached the meadow. The used pin remains on the wagon; spare pins stay in the kit for map assembly. A red ribbon flutters from the meadow's old animal waymarkers.

## Episode 8 · The Red Ribbon

**Story question:** Which of two old animal waymarkers leads to the last map section?

**Opening state:** Three missing sections have been recovered; one gap remains. C has brought the crew to the meadow. A red ribbon ties a folded cache note to a post between carved ram and rat waymarkers.

**New teaching:** Lowercase r represents the beginning sound of red. Review a and the final sounds in m/t. Model **ram**, then use **rat** as the meaningful new-word application. **Ran** is available for the journal. Do not add a vowel to the r model or turn it into a rolled sound.

**Sound bank:** red, ribbon, root, rope, rustle, river, room, rat, ram, ran. Rich words remain spoken; only the listed short words are child-reading targets.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `listening-1`: attend to the whole word, including the last sound, before choosing the waymarker.

### Scene progression

1. **Nearly there.** Nib spots the two carvings and starts toward the ram, pleased by its splendid curved horns. Lark calls him back to the folded note. The ribbon is simply a visible cache marker; it does not magically point to the correct animal. The note is dry and holds one clear word: **rat**.
2. **The beginning and the end.** Lark introduces r with red, ribbon, and root. Using the ram carving as a model, she blends **ram**. Then the child inspects the different final letter on the actual note. Nib expects another ram because the beginning looks the same. The final /t/ changes the word and the destination.
3. **A useful pause.** The child reads or recognizes **rat**, then touches the matching carving. The little drawer beneath its base opens with an ordinary latch. A wrong ram choice reveals an empty maintenance recess, not a penalty or a frightening encounter. Feedback returns to the note's last letter. Nib waits for the child to finish rather than charging ahead again.
4. **The last piece, not the last page.** D lies in the rat marker's dry drawer. Its hidden line runs toward a drawing of a crooked root. All four missing sections are now found. The crew keeps the red ribbon to bind its journal. They stop before trying to assemble the fragile paper in a gust. Nib suggests using their shelter and doing the job properly. The local search is finished; joining the map is tomorrow's job.

### Child turns

| Gate | Prompt and action | Kind | Feedback / narrated consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8.1 | Review **map** from its folio without an answer-revealing sound model | R | Help returns to the final /p/ after the attempt |
| 8.2 | Model r, then choose r among r and m | M | Contrast the beginning of red and map |
| 8.3 | Blend **ram** as a guided model while its carving is visible | M | Join all three sounds rather than identifying only the first letter |
| 8.4 | Reveal the note **rat**: “This word tells us which carving to use.” Decode with optional speech, or match the word to the animal after an attempt | A: new word from taught mappings | If the ram is chosen, point to final t and reblend; any supplied answer becomes supported participation |
| 8.5 | Open the rat marker's drawer and place D alongside the other sections | S | The fifth and final section is present; the completed inventory is visible |

**Dialogue anchors:** “The ram! Magnificent horns!” Nib exclaimed eagerly. “Magnificent. But what does the note say?” Lark asked cautiously. Later: “I was waiting for the little sound at the end,” Nib explained proudly.

**Staging:** Both carvings stand on fixed plinths at reachable heights. Their drawers belong visibly to the plinths. The red ribbon is tied to a real post, not suspended between layers. D is never shown already joined before the next chapter. Keep the rat friendly and stylized; there is no live animal threat.

**End state and handoff:** Original + A + B + C + D, red ribbon, all supplies, and eight completed journal chapters, including this one. The crew chooses a sheltered place to put the five map sections together.

## Episode 9 · The Map That Came Home

**Story question:** Why does the nearly finished map seem to lead nowhere—and can the crew join it without losing or tearing a piece?

**Opening state:** All five sections exist. One edge of D is folded underneath, hiding its connecting path. The crew has the mat, folios, umbrella, small sail, sand sack, spare pins, moonstone, and eight completed journal pages. No further map cache is needed.

**New teaching:** No new sound. Consolidate the cumulative set through practical reading, word contrasts, and aligning a sequence. Use **map/mat**, **pin/pan**, and selected **sit/sat/ran** according to actual support needs. The choices remain within the same 8–10 minute chapter; they are not an end-of-season exam.

**Sound bank:** Recur naturally through the familiar map, mat, pins, ribbon, sand sack, and ink. This chapter distributes review rather than pretending to have a new target sound.

**Essential-skill opportunity:** `planning-3`: align and secure paper carefully, revising the assembly when an edge does not fit.

### Scene progression

## Continuity and drafting checks

The written scripts can now be drafted in chapter order, using each brief's opening and end states as boundaries. Expand scene beats into narrated prose, connective action, and expressive conversation; do not inflate the story with an unrelated second problem to reach a word count. Keep gate prompts adjacent to their scene's visible goal and record their outcomes after the pause.

Before recording any draft, verify:

1. The five-section map ledger and the chapter count remain correct. Sections are recovered only in 2, 4, 6, and 8, and assembled in 9.
2. The next destination follows from an actual section, instruction, or solved problem. No new clue appears from nowhere at the start of an episode.
3. The child-reading targets stay within that chapter's taught mappings. An example supplied for performance must not leak an intended independent answer before its first response.
4. The modeled word and the response being measured are distinguished. Supported practice, word recognition, sound discrimination, oral reading, and story participation are not collapsed into one mastery claim.
5. Every character and prop has a physical support or an intentional movement cue, including transitions and boat travel.
6. The whole played chapter fits 8–10 minutes with normal child thinking and touch time. Exact word counts and audio timings belong to the drafted and recorded episode, not this planning document.
